Why should countries share a currency when they trade relatively little with one another and face different shocks? This paper studies a monetary balance-sheet channel: the pooling of foreign-exchange reserves can insure member countries against the monetary consequences of national sovereign-debt distress, even when it does not prevent sovereign default.

Using monthly IMF central-bank accounts for the eight WAEMU members from 2007 to 2026, the paper documents low comovement of national foreign claims (average pairwise correlation of 0.067) and a constructed regional coverage ratio about half as volatile as the national ones, with national and aggregate coverage diverging during the Malian sanctions and the Senegalese hidden-debt episode. A quantitative model of a small member economy with long-duration defaultable debt under a fixed parity shows that pooled backing reduces the mean quarterly devaluation probability from 1.60 to 0.29 percent and sharply weakens the pass-through of a national reserve loss to common coverage and the policy rate. This protection also changes the sovereign-debt equilibrium: external debt is higher (53.5 versus 45.7 percent of GDP) and defaults more frequent under pooling. Reserve pooling thus insures against devaluation, not against default.
