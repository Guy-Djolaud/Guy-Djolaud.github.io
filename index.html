About

My research is in labour economics and macroeconomics. I study how macroeconomic shocks reach Canadian households and labour markets, and how policy should respond.

A first strand examines employer market power: how employer concentration moves over the business cycle, who is most exposed to it, and how fiscal policy can mitigate its consequences.

A second strand asks how monetary policy should optimally respond to external shocks — foreign tariffs, commodity prices, and sovereign-debt distress in a monetary union.

I work with confidential Statistics Canada administrative microdata. I hold an active Statistics Canada security clearance and I am fully bilingual in French and English.

Research

Job market paper

Labour Market Power in Canada: Long-Term Trends and Cyclical Dynamics
Job market paper

The first time-series evidence on employer concentration in Canada: a gradual national decline, a sharp local rise in recession, and what both imply for wages and the labour share.

Paper Slides
Abstract

This paper provides the first time-series analysis of labour market concentration in Canada and proposes a novel approach to decomposing the underlying mechanisms. National concentration gradually declined from 2001 to 2019, while local concentration rose temporarily by 14 per cent during the Great Recession. The long-run decline reflects two offsetting forces: increased within-industry competition, which reduced concentration, and wage-bill shifts toward more concentrated sectors, which partially offset these gains. During the Great Recession the pattern reverses at the local level: 68 per cent of the increase was driven by sectoral reallocation. A calibrated TANK model with employer market power shows that fiscal expansions compress wage markdowns, increase the labour share and markedly reduce consumption and income inequality between workers and capitalists.

Phillips Curve Flattening Mechanically Weakens NAIRU Identification
Submitted August 2026

A flatter Phillips curve degrades NAIRU identification quadratically. On Canadian data, the credible interval for the unemployment gap contains zero in 97 per cent of quarters since 1991.

Paper
Abstract

In the standard random-walk state-space model, the likelihood information about an interior NAIRU state, relative to the smoothness precision supplied by the state equation, is proportional to the squared Phillips curve slope. Canadian quarterly data from 1977 to 2026 show the mechanism is quantitatively severe: across the February 1991 inflation-targeting break this ratio falls by a factor of twenty-three, driven almost entirely by the slope. The credible interval for the unemployment gap then contains zero in 97 per cent of quarters, against 73 per cent before, and without a sign restriction the posterior probability of a negative slope falls from 0.99 to 0.78. The result is analytical and holds for any state-space NAIRU model: a smooth posterior path is not evidence that the data locate the NAIRU.

Monetary policy and the open economy

When Tariffs Meet Oil: Optimal Monetary Policy with Household Debt
Working paper October 2026

When tariffs and oil prices rise together, optimal policy eases rather than tightens — and the choice of rule decides which households absorb the shock.

Paper Slides
Abstract

How should a commodity exporter set monetary policy when foreign tariffs and oil prices rise together, and who bears the adjustment? I study this question in a small open economy with nominal household debt, housing collateral and gradual mortgage repricing, calibrated to Canadian data. Under a CPI rule, the policy rate rises and borrowers absorb most of the contraction: their consumption falls by 1.1 per cent on impact, against 0.25 per cent for savers. The Ramsey allocation cuts the rate by 14 basis points, roughly halves the output decline and keeps borrower consumption at its no-shock level, at the cost of higher consumer price inflation. The policy choice is mainly redistributive, and faster mortgage repricing lets a smaller rate cut deliver larger immediate relief to indebted households.

The Insurance Value of Monetary Union: Sovereign Debt and Reserve Pooling
Working paper September 2026

Pooled foreign-exchange reserves insure member countries against devaluation risk — but not against sovereign default, and they raise equilibrium borrowing.

Paper
Abstract

Monetary union pools the foreign reserves that back a shared currency. I show that this pooling insures members against the monetary consequences of national sovereign-debt distress without insuring against default. Monthly central-bank accounts for the eight WAEMU members reveal weak comovement of national foreign claims and divergent national and area-wide reserve coverage during sovereign stress. In a quantitative model of a small member with long-duration defaultable debt, endogenous reserves, a coverage-dependent policy rate and a devaluation hazard, calibrated to Senegal, pooling lowers mean devaluation risk by 82 percent and attenuates the coverage loss from a national shock sevenfold, while equilibrium debt and default frequency rise.

Labour markets and the macroeconomy

The Trajectory of Immigrants' Exposure to Labour Market Power in Canada
Working paper with Yaya Diallo · CLEF Working Paper, Canadian Labour Economics Forum

Immigrants start out in the most concentrated corners of the Canadian labour market and move toward more competitive ones as they integrate.

Paper Slides
Abstract

This paper investigates the trajectory of labour market concentration among immigrants in the years following their arrival in Canada, using tax administrative data. We find that immigrants initially enter labour markets with higher concentrations of foreign-born workers. However, this concentration gradually declines over time, reflecting a transition toward more competitive and diverse employment environments as immigrants integrate. These findings contribute to a broader understanding of immigrant labour market assimilation and highlight the role of labour market power in shaping their employment trajectories.

Immigration, Labour Dynamics, and Fiscal Sustainability
Working paper with Fatim Diabagate

Immigration improves fiscal sustainability; skill complementarity and the age profile of arrivals matter most.

Paper
Abstract

This paper examines the implications of fiscal policy for debt sustainability following an immigration shock. When immigrants enter a country they work, consume and save, but they also age, fall ill and lose jobs, which requires government support. Using a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model featuring life cycle, death risk, skills and labour efficiency, calibrated on Canadian data, we simulate the equilibrium effect of immigration on sustainable debt. Whatever the driver considered, immigration improves fiscal solvency, and the largest changes come from the complementarity of immigrants' skills and their age profile.
